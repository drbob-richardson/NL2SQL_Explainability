\title{\textbf{Bayesian Active Graph Retrieval:\\ Structure as Covariance for Budget-Limited Multi-Hop RAG}\\[4pt]
\large\normalfont(expanded, hand-holding draft --- CS/ML paper of a two-paper split)}
\author{Robert Richardson}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation over multi-hop questions must often find a \emph{bridge} passage that is
semantically distant from the query yet essential to the reasoning chain. Dense retrievers bury it, and
verifying candidates with a large language model (LLM) is expensive, so retrieval under a judgment budget
becomes a problem of Bayesian experimental design. We argue that the corpus \emph{graph}---useless as a ranking
prior, since diffusion buries the answer---is load-bearing as a \emph{covariance}: relevance is correlated
along graph edges, so one paid judgment on a findable anchor propagates belief to its buried, connected
neighbours. We place a Gaussian-process prior on a latent relevance field whose mean is the semantic retriever
and whose covariance is a graph-Laplacian kernel, and we select judgments by optimistic acquisition. Three
findings organize the paper: a \emph{correlation-form} graph kernel is essential; the advantage is realized
through \emph{exploration}, not a myopic value-of-information rule; and the method's value obeys a sharp
\emph{alignment law}---it helps in proportion to how well the graph encodes the true evidence dependencies,
which we make measurable and validate across benchmarks where it holds and one, adversarially constructed, where
the required surface-graph alignment fails.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{The problem in one paragraph}
A \emph{multi-hop} question is one whose answer requires chaining several facts, each living in a different
passage. Consider ``\emph{Who is the spouse of the performer of the song Green?}'' Answering it requires (1)
finding \emph{who performed ``Green''}---say, an artist $X$---and then (2) finding \emph{$X$'s spouse}. The
passage about $X$ is the linchpin, yet it is semantically about $X$, not about ``spouses'' or ``Green,'' so a
similarity-based retriever ranks it low. We call such a linchpin a \emph{bridge}. Retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) systems that feed a fixed top-$k$ to a reader routinely drop the bridge and therefore fail.

One can repair this by \emph{verifying} candidates with an LLM: ask ``is this passage relevant to the
question?'' But each such call is expensive, so a system can afford only a small budget $B$ of them per query.
The question this paper answers is: \emph{given that budget, which candidates should we pay to verify so that
the passages we ultimately hand the reader contain the whole evidence chain?}

\subsection{The thesis: semantics sets the mean, structure sets the dependence}
A natural idea is to use a graph over passages (who links to / mentions whom) to help retrieve. The obvious way
---let relevance ``flow'' along edges and re-rank by the diffused score---backfires for multi-hop: diffusion
spreads mass to topically-central neighbours and buries the specific bridge even further. That failure is well
documented and is the starting point of this work.

Our move is to use the graph not to say \emph{where} relevance is, but to say \emph{where relevance is
correlated}. Formally we treat relevance as a random field over the candidate pool and let the graph be its
\emph{covariance structure}. Under a verification budget this is exactly the useful object: if two passages are
graph-connected, then verifying one is informative about the other, even if they are far apart in embedding
space. So one paid judgment on a \emph{findable} anchor updates our belief about its \emph{buried but connected}
bridge. The graph earns its keep as covariance, not as a re-ranker.

A word on slogans, because it matters to a Bayesian reader: a GP covariance \emph{is} part of the prior, so the
contrast is not ``covariance versus prior.'' The precise statement is \emph{mean versus dependence}:
\textbf{semantics determines the prior mean, and structure determines the prior covariance (the dependence).}
Equivalently, ``structure as dependence, not as relevance''---the graph never asserts \emph{that} a passage is
relevant, only \emph{with what} its relevance co-varies.

\begin{intuition}
``Prior'' answers \emph{where do I expect relevance to be?} (the dense retriever's job). ``Covariance'' answers
\emph{if I learn one passage's relevance, what else does that teach me?} (the graph's job). The multi-hop bridge
is where these diverge: low prior (semantically far) but high covariance with a findable anchor (structurally
near). Spend your scarce verifications where they teach you the most, and let the covariance carry the lesson to
the bridge.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Model.} A Gaussian process over latent relevance with a \emph{semantic mean} and a \emph{graph
  covariance}, and the (initially surprising) finding that the graph kernel must be used in \emph{correlation
  (unit-diagonal) form} to be effective. \TESTED{normalization win, \S\ref{sec:model}}
  \item \textbf{Acquisition.} Evidence that the structural gain flows through optimistic \emph{exploration}
  (upper-confidence-bound selection), and that a principled set-completion value-of-information rule
  \emph{destroys} it. \TESTED{\S\ref{sec:acq}}
  \item \textbf{Alignment law.} A characterization---structure helps iff the graph aligns with the reasoning
  chain---made measurable by a scalar (\emph{gold-connectivity}), with an oracle ceiling, validated positively
  on HotpotQA/2WikiMultiHopQA and as a sharp boundary on the adversarial MuSiQue. \TESTED{\S\ref{sec:law}}
  \item \textbf{Adaptivity (proposed).} A hierarchical mixture kernel with a learned query-specific weight, and
  an \emph{adaptive} graph inferred from the first judgments. \PROP{\S\ref{sec:mixture},\,\S\ref{sec:future}}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}
This section defines, from the ground up, every technical object the paper uses. Readers fluent in Gaussian
processes and Bayesian optimization can skim, but the framing (especially the graph kernel and the correlation
form) is worth confirming.

\subsection{Dense retrieval, embeddings, and cosine similarity}
An \emph{embedding model} is a map $\text{enc}:\text{text}\to\R^d$ (we use $d=1536$) trained so that
semantically similar texts have vectors pointing in similar directions. \emph{Cosine similarity}
$\cos(u,v)=\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}\in[-1,1]$ measures that alignment. \emph{First-stage (dense)
retrieval} embeds the query and every passage and ranks passages by $\cos(\text{query},\text{passage})$; the
top-$N$ form the \emph{candidate pool} we work inside. Embeddings capture topical/semantic similarity well but
have no notion of multi-step reasoning---hence the bridge problem.

\subsection{The multi-hop bridge, precisely}
\begin{example}[running example]\label{ex:run}
Query $q=$ ``spouse of the performer of Green.'' Pool $D_q=\{d_1,\dots,d_N\}$. The gold (required) set is
$R_q=\{d_A,d_B\}$ where $d_A$ is ``$X$ performed \emph{Green}'' (the \textbf{bridge}: names the intermediate
entity $X$) and $d_B$ is ``$X$'s spouse is $Y$'' (\textbf{direct evidence}: contains the answer $Y$). Because
$q$ mentions ``spouse'' and ``Green,'' $\cos(q,d_B)$ is moderate but $\cos(q,d_A)$ is low---$d_A$ is about $X$,
whom the query never names. The retriever finds $d_B$ but buries $d_A$. If the corpus graph links $d_A$ and
$d_B$ (e.g., $d_B$'s text mentions $X$, $d_A$'s title), then verifying the findable $d_B$ can, through the
covariance, lift the buried $d_A$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Gaussian processes on a finite set (= a multivariate normal)}
A \emph{Gaussian process} (GP) is a distribution over functions such that any finite set of function values is
jointly Gaussian. Because our index set is the finite pool $\{1,\dots,N\}$, the GP here is nothing more exotic
than an $N$-dimensional multivariate normal. We posit a latent \emph{relevance score} vector $f\in\R^N$,
\begin{equation}
  f \sim \N(m,\,K),\qquad m\in\R^N,\ K\in\R^{N\times N}\ \text{symmetric positive semidefinite,}
\end{equation}
where $m_i$ is our prior mean belief about passage $i$'s relevance and $K_{ij}$ is the prior covariance between
the relevances of $i$ and $j$. Larger $f_i$ means ``more relevant''; we will rank by (the posterior mean of)
$f$. The two ingredients---mean $m$ and covariance $K$---are the entire modeling surface, and the thesis is
about who supplies each: the retriever supplies $m$, the graph supplies $K$.

\subsection{Kernels: what a covariance matrix encodes}
A \emph{kernel} is a function producing the covariance matrix $K$. It encodes \emph{which passages we expect to
share relevance}. Two candidates:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Embedding kernel} $K_E(i,j)=\exp\!\big(-(1-\cos(v_i,v_j))/\ell\big)$: passages with similar
  embeddings have correlated relevance. Length-scale $\ell>0$ sets how quickly correlation decays with
  embedding distance. \emph{This is the assumption of embedding-space active learning; multi-hop violates it.}
  \item \textbf{Graph kernel} (next subsection): passages that are \emph{connected in the corpus graph} have
  correlated relevance---even if far in embedding space.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Graphs, the Laplacian, and the GMRF/CAR kernel}\label{sec:gmrf}
Let $A\in\{0,1\}^{N\times N}$ be a symmetric \emph{adjacency matrix} on the pool ($A_{ij}=1$ if passages $i,j$
are ``linked''; we discuss what ``linked'' means in \S\ref{sec:law}). Let $D=\mathrm{diag}(A\mathbf 1)$ be the
\emph{degree} matrix and define the \emph{graph Laplacian}
\begin{equation}
  L \;=\; D - A .
\end{equation}
Its defining property is the quadratic form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lapquad}
  x^\top L x \;=\; \tfrac12\sum_{i,j} A_{ij}\,(x_i-x_j)^2 \;=\; \sum_{(i,j)\in\text{edges}}(x_i-x_j)^2,
\end{equation}
which is small when $x$ varies \emph{smoothly} across edges and large when connected nodes disagree. We build a
covariance from it via a \emph{Gaussian Markov random field} (GMRF), a.k.a.\ a proper conditional
autoregressive (CAR) model:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kg}
  g \sim \N\!\big(0,\,K_G\big),\qquad K_G = (I+\lambda L)^{-1}\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \
  p(g)\propto \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12\,g^\top (I+\lambda L)\,g\Big).
\end{equation}
Here the \emph{precision} (inverse covariance) is $I+\lambda L$: the $\lambda L$ term rewards graph-smoothness
via \eqref{eq:lapquad}, and the identity $I$ makes the precision invertible (a \emph{proper} prior; a bare $L$
is singular because it has the constant vector in its null space). The parameter $\lambda>0$ tunes how strongly
connectivity couples relevances.

\begin{intuition}
A GMRF is the graph analogue of a smoothing prior. \eqref{eq:kg} says: ``I believe relevance is a smooth
function on the graph.'' The inverse $(I+\lambda L)^{-1}$ then has large off-diagonal entries exactly between
connected nodes---so \emph{learning one connected node's value shifts my belief about the other}. That
belief-transfer is the propagation the whole method rides on. Crucially, this uses the graph as
\emph{covariance} (how beliefs co-move), which is completely different from using it to diffuse a score.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Active learning, Bayesian optimization, and acquisition rules}\label{sec:bo}
We can \emph{query} (verify/judge) a candidate to reveal a noisy label of its relevance, at unit cost, up to a
budget $B$. Choosing \emph{which} to query to best inform a goal is \emph{active learning} / \emph{Bayesian
optimization}. Two ways to score a candidate $i$ for querying:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Upper Confidence Bound (UCB):} $a_{\mathrm{UCB}}(i)=\mu_i+\beta\,s_i$, the posterior mean plus a
  multiple of the posterior standard deviation. It trades off \emph{exploitation} (high mean $\mu_i$) and
  \emph{exploration} (high uncertainty $s_i$). Standard in bandits and BO; the constant $\beta$ sets optimism.
  \item \textbf{Expected Value of Information (EVOI):} pick the query whose expected outcome most reduces the
  \emph{Bayes risk} of the final decision. This is decision-theoretically ``correct'' one step ahead---but, as
  we will see, correct-looking is not the same as effective here.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Setup}\label{sec:setup}
For a query $q$ let $D_q=\{d_1,\dots,d_N\}$ ($N{=}100$) be the candidate pool, each with embedding $v_i$. Let
$r_i\in\{0,1\}$ indicate membership in the unknown required evidence set $R_q$, with $|R_q|=k$ (for a $k$-hop
question, $k$ passages). A \emph{judgment} on $d_i$ costs one unit and returns a noisy relevance label $y_i$
from an LLM oracle; the budget is $B$ judgments. Having judged a set $S$ (with $|S|\le B$), the system returns a
\emph{context} $\widehat R$ of size $k$ to the reader. The natural loss is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
  \mathcal L(\widehat R, R_q) \;=\; \underbrace{c_{\text{miss}}\,\mathbf 1\{R_q\not\subseteq \widehat R\}}_{\text{missed any required passage}} \;+\; \underbrace{c_{\text{ctx}}\,|\widehat R|}_{\text{context cost}} .
\end{equation}
With $|\widehat R|$ fixed to $k$, minimizing $\E[\mathcal L]$ reduces to maximizing the probability of
\emph{chain completion}, $\Pr(R_q\subseteq\widehat R)$: getting the \emph{whole} chain, not merely more of it.
We report recall@$k$ (fraction of $R_q$ retrieved), chain completion $\mathbf 1\{R_q\subseteq\widehat R\}$, and
downstream answer exact-match (EM) and token-$F_1$.

\begin{intuition}
Why insist on the \emph{whole} chain? Because a $2$-hop answer needs \emph{both} the bridge and the direct
evidence; retrieving one of two is often as useless as retrieving zero. This is why average recall understates
the intervention and chain completion is the honest target (\S\ref{sec:law}).
\end{intuition}

\section{Model: Structure as Covariance}\label{sec:model}
\subsection{The latent relevance field}
We model a latent relevance score $f_q\in\R^N$ as a GP with a semantic mean and a structural covariance,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:field}
  f_q \;=\; m_q + g_q, \qquad g_q \sim \gp\!\big(0,\, K_q\big), \qquad m_q(i) = \sigma\!\big(a\,\cos(q,d_i)+b\big),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $(a,b)$ are fit \emph{once, pooled across queries}, mapping cosine
to a calibrated relevance probability (a cross-query prior; see \S\ref{sec:mixture}). The decomposition
$f=m+g$ is the crux: $m_q$ (the retriever) says where relevance is expected; $g_q$ (mean zero, graph covariance)
is the \emph{correlated residual}---the part of relevance the retriever misses but that travels along the graph.
We rank by the posterior mean of $f$; equivalently one may read $r_i=\mathbf 1\{f_i>\tau\}$ as a probit model.

\begin{intuition}
Read \eqref{eq:field} as ``relevance $=$ what the retriever thinks $+$ a graph-smooth correction.'' The retriever
is right on average but blind to bridges; the correction $g$ is where the graph gets to speak, and it only
speaks in terms of \emph{correlations}, never overriding the mean until a judgment gives it evidence.
\end{intuition}

\subsection{Two kernels, and why the embedding kernel is wrong here}
With $K_E,K_G$ as in \S\ref{sec:prelim}--\ref{sec:gmrf}: the embedding kernel assumes ``close in embedding
$\Rightarrow$ correlated relevance.'' Our running Example~\ref{ex:run} is a direct counterexample: the bridge
$d_A$ is \emph{far} from the direct-evidence $d_B$ in embedding space (different topics---an artist vs.\ a
spouse), yet their relevances are perfectly coupled (both required, and only both). The graph kernel encodes
exactly that coupling if $d_A,d_B$ are linked. This is the formal sense in which multi-hop breaks the embedding
assumption and calls for a graph covariance.

\subsection{The load-bearing detail: correlation (unit-diagonal) form}
We use \emph{both} kernels in unit-diagonal (correlation) form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:norm}
  \Kt(i,j) \;=\; \frac{K(i,j)}{\sqrt{K(i,i)\,K(j,j)}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Kt(i,i)=1\ \ \forall i.
\end{equation}
For the embedding kernel this is a no-op ($K_E$ already has unit diagonal). For the graph kernel it is decisive.
Here is why, concretely. The raw GMRF covariance $K_G=(I+\lambda L)^{-1}$ has a \emph{non-constant} diagonal:
$K_G(i,i)$---the prior variance of node $i$'s relevance---is \emph{smaller} for high-degree ``hub'' nodes,
because a hub is pinned down by many smoothness constraints. Now recall the UCB rule
$a(i)=\mu_i+\beta s_i$: it explores nodes with large posterior standard deviation $s_i$. Under the raw kernel,
hubs start with \emph{small} variance, so UCB \emph{under-explores} them---but hubs are precisely the
well-connected anchors whose judgment would propagate the furthest. Rescaling to unit diagonal
\eqref{eq:norm} equalizes the prior variances, so exploration is driven by the \emph{correlation structure}
(who is connected to whom) rather than by the accident of node degree.

\begin{intuition}
The raw graph kernel quietly whispers ``don't bother checking the hubs, you already know them''---the opposite
of what you want, since checking a hub is what lights up its whole neighbourhood. Normalizing removes that
whisper. It is a one-line change with an outsized effect because it interacts with the exploration rule
(\S\ref{sec:acq}).
\end{intuition}
\TESTED{On chain completion at $N{=}100$ under a real LLM judge, normalizing the graph kernel improves
graph$-$cosine by $+0.05/+0.09/+0.12$ at budgets $B{=}1/2/3$ (paired 95\% CIs exclude 0); the graph$-$cosine
margin roughly \emph{doubles} from $+0.068$ to $+0.12$--$0.15$. It is the single largest free improvement we found.}

\paragraph{This is a finding, not an invention.} Normalizing a graph kernel to equal per-vertex prior variance
is a known operation (local/correlation normalization of graph kernels; Urry \& Sollich, 2013). Our contribution
is not the operator but the \emph{finding} that it is \emph{load-bearing for active graph retrieval}: without it,
the very hubs that carry the most structural information look artificially certain, and an optimistic acquisition
skips them---so a routine normalization interacts decisively with exploration and graph degree. We present it as
a finding about that interaction, and do not headline ``unit-diagonal kernels'' as a method.

\subsection{Conditioning on a judgment: the posterior}
Suppose we have judged the set $S$ and observed labels $y_S$. Because everything is Gaussian, the posterior over
$f$ is Gaussian with closed form. Writing $K$ for the (normalized) kernel and $\sigma^2$ for observation noise,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:post}
  \mu \;=\; m + K_{\cdot S}\,(K_{SS}+\sigma^2 I)^{-1}(y_S - m_S), \qquad
  s^2 \;=\; \mathrm{diag}\!\Big(K - K_{\cdot S}(K_{SS}+\sigma^2 I)^{-1}K_{S\cdot}\Big),
\end{equation}
where $K_{\cdot S}$ is the $N\times|S|$ block of covariances between all nodes and the judged nodes. Read
\eqref{eq:post} slowly: the update to node $i$'s mean is $\sum_{j\in S} [\,\text{coupling of }i\text{ to }j\,]
\times[\,\text{surprise at }j\,]$, where ``surprise'' is $y_j-m_j$ (how much the judgment differed from the
prior) and ``coupling'' comes from the kernel. For the graph kernel, the coupling of a buried bridge to a
judged anchor is large, so a surprising ``relevant!'' at the anchor \emph{raises the bridge's posterior mean}.
That is propagation, made arithmetic.

\subsection{Why the update must be soft (noisy), not hard}
A tempting shortcut is to treat a judgment as ground truth: if the judge says ``relevant,'' pin $f_i=+\infty$;
if ``irrelevant,'' pin $f_i=-\infty$. This is the $\sigma^2\to0$ limit and it is a disaster under a real judge.
LLM judges are wrong often, and \emph{systematically} so (they miss bridges---see the companion Paper B). A hard
pin on a mistaken ``irrelevant'' \emph{sinks} a true bridge, and worse, the graph then \emph{propagates} that
error to the bridge's neighbours. Keeping $\sigma^2>0$ treats the label as evidence to be weighed against the
prior and the neighbours, so a single wrong ``no'' is outvoted rather than obeyed.
\TESTED{Under a real judge, the hard-pin design collapses (the graph advantage goes significantly negative);
the soft update removes the collapse. The right $\sigma^2$ reflects judge reliability---formalized in Paper B.}

\subsection{Proposed: a hierarchical mixture kernel}\label{sec:mixture}
The graph is not equally valuable for every query (\S\ref{sec:law}). We therefore propose a per-query convex
combination of the two normalized kernels,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mix}
  K_q(\lambda_q) = (1-\lambda_q)\,\Kt_E + \lambda_q\,\Kt_G, \qquad \lambda_q=\sigma(w^\top\phi_q)\in[0,1],
\end{equation}
where $\phi_q$ are cheap, gold-free query features (how buried the top candidates are, how dense the graph is,
whether the first judgment agreed with the prior). Because a single query offers only $B\le3$ judgments---far
too few to fit $\lambda_q$ on its own---we place a \emph{hierarchical} prior on $w$ and pool across queries.
This is the same ``borrow strength across queries'' device that stabilizes cold-start elsewhere, now deciding
\emph{how much to trust the graph} for each query.
\begin{intuition}
On an ``easy'' query where the retriever already nails both hops, the graph is a liability (it can only add
noise), so $\lambda_q\to0$; on a ``buried, connected'' query it is the whole game, so $\lambda_q\to1$. Learning
$\lambda_q$ turns the fragile ``sometimes-helps-sometimes-hurts'' behaviour into a controlled, query-adaptive
decision.
\end{intuition}
\PARTIAL{\textbf{Status (now run).} An oracle-$\lambda$ ceiling (best fixed $\lambda$ per query, in hindsight)
shows a modest residual headroom ($+0.03$--$0.045$) over the fixed graph kernel, and gold-free features \emph{do}
predict the per-query graph advantage (top-$k$ cosine negatively---a confident prior means the graph adds less).
\emph{But} a learned gate (5-fold CV, gold-free features) merely \emph{ties} the fixed graph kernel out-of-sample
on \emph{chained-only} data ($-0.005$): the graph helps $\sim$86\% of chained queries, so there is almost nothing
to route, and the oracle headroom is itself small and budget-inconsistent. $\lambda_q$'s value needs a
\emph{mixed} query distribution including graph-\emph{unfavorable} (independent/comparison) queries where
$\lambda_q\to0$; showing it requires those queries judged, which we have not done. So the learned gate is
\emph{feasible and sensibly-signalled but not yet a demonstrated win}---an honest open item, not a result.}

\section{Active Retrieval: Acquisition and the Exploration Mechanism}\label{sec:acq}
The algorithm is: repeat $B$ times \{choose the highest-acquisition unjudged candidate, judge it, update
\eqref{eq:post}\}; then return the top-$k$ of the posterior mean $\mu$. The only design choice is the
acquisition rule. We compared two.

\paragraph{UCB (optimistic exploration).} $a_{\mathrm{UCB}}(i)=\mu_i+\beta s_i$ (\S\ref{sec:bo}). It judges the
candidate that is either promising or uncertain.

\paragraph{Decision-aware value of information (two forms).} Since the decision is to return the top-$k$, one
can instead judge the candidate whose observation most reduces the risk of \emph{failing to complete the
chain}. We distinguish two forms, and the distinction matters:
\begin{align}
  U_{\text{mass}} &= \sum_{j\notin\widehat R}\Pr(r_j=1) &&\text{(expected \emph{number} of relevant passages omitted --- a surrogate),}\\
  U_{\text{cc}} &= 1 - \prod_{j\notin\widehat R}\big(1-\Pr(r_j=1)\big) &&\text{(probability that \emph{any} relevant passage is omitted).}\label{eq:ccfail}
\end{align}
In both, we judge $\arg\max_i\big(U-\E_{y_i}[U\mid\text{judge }i]\big)$. The second, \eqref{eq:ccfail}, is the
\emph{genuine} one-step Bayes-risk reduction for our objective $\Pr(R_q\subseteq\widehat R)$, not a surrogate;
we compute its expectation by a two-point posterior-predictive over $y_i$. Testing it is what licenses (or
would deny) any claim about ``decision-aware acquisition.''

\paragraph{The finding: exploration beats even the correct one-step VOI.}
\TESTED{On the (normalized) graph kernel, \emph{both} decision-aware rules lose to UCB, significantly and at
every budget. The true completion-VOI \eqref{eq:ccfail} trails graph-UCB by $-0.078/-0.102/-0.113$ in chain
completion at $B{=}1/2/3$ (paired 95\% CIs exclude 0), essentially tying the omitted-mass surrogate; the same
VOI \emph{helps} the embedding kernel, and under VOI the graph-vs-cosine advantage vanishes.}
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
chain completion @$B{=}1$ (pooled, normalized) & UCB & VOI ($U_{\text{mass}}$) & VOI ($U_{\text{cc}}$) \\ \midrule
embedding (cosine) kernel & 0.323 & --- & 0.335 \\
graph kernel  & \textbf{0.445} & 0.362 & 0.367 \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{intuition}
Why does even the \emph{correct} one-step rule lose? The graph's payoff is a \emph{two-stage} play: judge the
confident \emph{anchor} now, and the covariance surfaces the \emph{bridge} at the \emph{next} decision. A
one-step VOI values only the immediate change in completion risk, and judging the anchor barely moves that risk
this turn (the anchor is already inside the top-$k$), so the myopic rule spends the budget elsewhere and never
triggers the propagation. UCB, indifferent to the immediate decision, happily judges the high-uncertainty
hub---the move that pays off a stage later. \textbf{So this is not ``a bad surrogate backfires'': myopic
Bayes-optimal acquisition is genuinely inferior here because the value of graph exploration is multi-stage.}
Characterizing that gap---one-step VOI versus the optimal sequential (POMDP) policy---is a theoretical opening
this paper flags rather than closes.
\end{intuition}

\section{When Does Structure Help? The Alignment Law}\label{sec:law}
Everything so far assumed a graph $A$. This section asks: what must $A$ be, and when does any of this work?

\subsection{What ``linked'' means}
We instantiate $A$ with a \emph{title-mention} graph: $A_{ij}=1$ if passage $j$'s title (its subject entity)
appears in passage $i$'s text. For Example~\ref{ex:run}, the direct-evidence passage $d_B$ (about $X$'s spouse)
mentions $X$, whose article is the bridge $d_A$---so $A_{AB}=1$ and the covariance can carry belief from $d_B$
to $d_A$. This graph is free (no model calls) and, for standard multi-hop corpora, aligns with the reasoning.

\subsection{Structural leverage: the algebra of ``does a judgment help?''}
How much does judging $i$ reduce our uncertainty about $j$? For a GP, observing $i$ shrinks $j$'s posterior
variance by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lev}
  \Delta_{i\to j} \;\propto\; \frac{K(i,j)^2}{K(i,i)+\sigma^2}.
\end{equation}
Read off the conditions for the graph to matter: we surface a buried gold $j$ (small $m_j$, so we would
otherwise miss it) precisely when there is an \emph{observable} anchor $i$ (large $m_i$, so UCB judges it early)
with \emph{large} $K_G(i,j)$ (graph-connected) and \emph{tolerable} noise $\sigma^2$ (a reliable-enough judge).
This is the equation behind every empirical result in the paper.

\subsection{Gold-connectivity: making ``alignment'' measurable}
\begin{definition}[Gold-connectivity --- an \emph{explanatory} (oracle) statistic]
For a query with gold set $R_q$, the alignment score is $\mathbf 1\{\exists\,i\neq j\in R_q:\ A_{ij}=1\}$
(more finely, the count of within-$R_q$ edges): \emph{is the required chain connected in the graph?} If the
golds are disconnected, \eqref{eq:lev} offers no anchor-to-bridge path and the graph cannot help.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Oracle vs.\ operational alignment (an important distinction).} Gold-connectivity uses the true
$R_q$, so it is an \emph{explanatory}, oracle diagnostic: it tells us \emph{why} the method succeeds or fails,
but it cannot be computed for a live query. An \emph{operational} alignment predictor must be gold-free---built
from candidate burial (how deep the plausible anchors sit), graph density and anchor concentration, and the
agreement between the first judgments and the prior. Learning to predict from such features \emph{how much to
trust the graph} is precisely the job of the mixture weight $\lambda_q$ (\S\ref{sec:mixture}); the oracle-$\lambda$
ceiling shows the target is worth chasing, and \emph{closing the gap between the explanatory statistic and an
operational, cross-validated predictor is a primary next step}. We are careful, throughout, not to claim
gold-connectivity is something one measures a priori---it is not; it is the lens that explains the boundary.

\PROP{\textbf{Alignment law (informal, to be proven).} The expected reduction in set-completion loss from the
graph kernel, relative to the embedding kernel, is non-decreasing in gold-connectivity and vanishes when the
required chain is disconnected. The clean statement---and its proof under a block-correlation model in which
gold passages form one correlated block and distractors another---is the paper's main theoretical to-do.}

\subsection{The empirical picture: a positive regime and a boundary}
\TESTED{\textbf{Positive (surface graph suffices).} On HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA, the title-mention graph
has gold-connectivity $0.69$--$0.76$, and the graph kernel beats the embedding kernel (BAGEL-lite) and passive
verify-top-$B$ on chain completion by $+0.12$--$0.15$ at low budget (both datasets), and on downstream answer
$F_1$ by $+0.040$ (pooled and on Hotpot; 2Wiki borderline). The advantage is a \emph{low-budget} phenomenon and
decays as $B$ grows and all methods converge.}

\TESTED{\textbf{Boundary (adversarial).} MuSiQue is constructed so distractors are surface-similar to golds. There
the title graph's gold-connectivity is only $0.23$--$0.53$, and \emph{no cheap graph recovers the chain}: an
entity-overlap graph reconnects the golds ($0.23\!\to\!0.86$) but is too dense (diffuse propagation); a
sparsity sweep, a question-conditioned graph, a prior-gated graph, and an LLM-\emph{decomposition} graph (which
is both sparse and $0.66$--$0.78$ gold-connected) all give $\approx0$ margin, because matching sub-questions to
passages still relies on cosine---the very signal MuSiQue defeats. Yet an \emph{oracle} gold-clique graph
restores $+0.07$--$0.09$ (including $3$-hop), so the \emph{mechanism is sound}; the binding constraint is chain
\emph{identification}, not the covariance idea.}

\begin{intuition}
MuSiQue is the control experiment that proves the thesis by breaking it in the predicted way. Give the method a
graph that matches the reasoning (the oracle clique) and it delivers; give it only surface signals on a corpus
engineered to make surface signals lie, and it cannot build that graph---but nothing about the covariance
machinery is at fault. The lesson is a \emph{law}: structure-as-covariance helps cheaply iff the corpus's
surface structure aligns with the reasoning chain---a property whose oracle form (gold-connectivity) explains
the outcome after the fact, and whose gold-free predictor is the operational target we build toward via
$\lambda_q$.
\end{intuition}

\section{Related Work}
\emph{Active / Bayesian retrieval and relevance feedback}; \emph{BAGEL} (query-specific GP-UCB over passage
embeddings---our nearest neighbour, and the kernel our data refute); \emph{GraphRAG} and \emph{HopRAG}
(graph-structured retrieval; HopRAG builds logical pseudo-query edges precisely because surface similarity is
insufficient---consonant with our alignment law); \emph{GMRF/CAR priors on graphs} (Rue \& Held); \emph{Bayesian
optimization and value of information}; and \emph{set-wise retrieval} (SETR), which argues for returning a set
rather than a ranking. Our distinction: the graph enters as the \emph{covariance of a latent relevance field with
a semantic mean}, not as a ranking signal or a zero-mean embedding kernel, and we characterize precisely
\emph{when} it helps. Notably, BAGEL's own paper names two limitations---dependence on the embedding
representation / kernel match, and the danger that erroneous LLM relevance scores are propagated confidently by
the GP. Our two moves address exactly these: graph \emph{dependence} replaces the mismatched embedding kernel,
and the \emph{soft} (noisy-likelihood) update prevents confident propagation of a wrong label. We therefore
position the paper as a targeted sequel, not a reinvention of graph GPs or active retrieval---the novelty is the
mean/dependence decomposition, the exploration mechanism, and the alignment regime, not any single primitive.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}\label{sec:future}
The effects are modest and low-budget; the win is over a re-implemented BAGEL; two of three benchmarks are
standard multi-hop. The deepest open direction is \PROP{\textbf{adaptive structure learning}: instead of fixing
$A$ up front, \emph{infer} it from the first few judgments. Each judgment reveals not only whether a passage is
relevant but---if we ask the judge the right question---\emph{which hop} it serves, so the oracle's own
reasoning can construct the chain graph within the budget. This unifies the two roles of structure (covariance
here; identification in Paper B) and is the most plausible route to helping on adversarial corpora without a
cheating oracle.}

\section{Conclusion}
The corpus graph, worthless for ranking, is load-bearing as a covariance for budget-limited multi-hop
retrieval---provided it is used in correlation form, driven by optimistic exploration, and aligned with the
reasoning chain. We give the model, the mechanism, and a measurable law for its applicability, positive where
surface structure suffices and honestly bounded where an adversarial corpus breaks that alignment.
